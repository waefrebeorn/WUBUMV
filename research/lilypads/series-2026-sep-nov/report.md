### 3. Stickocalypse — @Stickocalypse_Shorts (YouTube Shorts)

- **What it is:** a zombie-survival series told in parts with stick figures, side view, flat-colour sets with fog, third-person narration, burned-in captions. Five videos; Part 1 "The Last Farm" **4.2 million views**; Part 2 270K; Part 4 142K; Part 5 60K; **35,600 subscribers** (checked Oct 19, 2026).
- **Why best-in-window:** the cleanest measured case in the window of *format beating footage*. The write-up that took it apart (vuela.ai, Oct 2026) transcribed every episode and found one skeleton: a counterintuitive second-person hook ("if the zombie apocalypse starts while you're on a farm"), the main character as viewer-stand-in, a practical guide dressed as story (30 minutes before the grid fails, water first, the well runs on an electric pump), concrete numbers (30 chickens, 12 cattle, one horse, a dog), one-word rule lines ("Don't", "Nothing", "Silence"), the mistake paid in the *next* part (the generator noise in Part 1 brings the neighbour in Part 2), and an ending that is a concrete threat already inside the fence — never "what would you do?". Editing: a new shot every 2–3 seconds, rotating map / wide / close-up / object-insert / over-the-shoulder, at least three settings per episode.
- **Audience / cadence / format:** YouTube Shorts, 60–100 s episodes; the Part 1 → Part 2 drop (4.2M → 270K) is the format's own lesson: Part 1 is the one the algorithm shows strangers; later parts live on returners — **every part needs its own hook even when it continues the story**.
- **Lesson for the 5 shows:** **the hook is a threat, not a question — and the device is the edit.** Three lifts, directly: (1) *every 10s unit needs its own hook* — the shows' units already have one spoken line each at 7–9s; make that line the threat/turn, not a greeting; (2) *concrete numbers make the unbelievable believable* — SHOW-4 THE COUNT's whole premise, but the numbers should be counted *in the scene* (30 chickens, not "many"); (3) *code beats generation for a repeated system* — the write-up's own test: AI video warped the stick figures; code animation held them identical across shots. The 5 shows' transition devices are code-shaped ideas (a carry, a count, a name); where a device must survive 4 units unchanged, a code-rendered or still-based element is safer than a generated one. Also: the map shot that returns every time the place changes is SHOW-2 THE TENTH SECOND's carry, drawn as a map.
- **Sources:** vuela.ai, "How to Make Viral Stick Figure Series with AI: the Stickocalypse Case" (checked Oct 19, 2026); youtube.com/@Stickocalypse_Shorts.

## The single biggest lesson

**The device is the show; the episode number is the retention.** Across every tier — a 30-episode studio microdrama, a 62-episode Facebook soap, a 4.2M-view stick-figure series, a 3-episode horror event, and a Prime Video premiere — the series that work are the ones where a *repeatable, visible device* survives every cut (a carry, a count, a name, a handoff, a map) and where the audience is waiting on a *numbered next part*, not a pretty clip. The ones that fail (Castle Walls) have neither: no device, no episode engine, just a cinematic look. The 5 shows already have the devices. The work now is the numbering, the cadence promise, and the refusal to let any unit be merely beautiful.
